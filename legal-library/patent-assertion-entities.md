# Patent assertion against operating companies

The Patent Act supplies injunctions, damages, and the cost of defense. Non-practicing entities that exist to assert patents convert that package into licensing revenue and settlements, especially against high-tech operating companies. Individual inventors and small operating firms face the same statute with worse win rates, higher invalidation risk, and less ability to threaten a costly defense.

## Outcome / Goal

The practical result is transfer of money from operating companies (and their customers, via prices) to patent assertion entities and their funders, often without a merits judgment. The parties who obtain it are PAEs, related NPEs, and litigation-investment entities. Large operating companies both pay and use the same instruments; resource asymmetry still decides who can afford to finish a case.

## Contrast / Parallel Track

A practicing company suing a competitor over a product it makes is the textbook infringement case. An individual inventor suing with a narrow patent and no war chest is formally allowed to do the same thing. In practice, small patentees have lower win rates and higher invalidation rates under 35 U.S.C. § 101 and other validity doctrines, while PAEs with portfolios, favorable venues, and the ability to impose discovery costs extract settlements that are cheaper than defense. The closed analog is not "patents do not exist for small parties"; it is that the assertion business model is available mainly to parties who can impose and survive litigation cost.

## Formal Legal Instruments

| Instrument | Role in the outcome | Citation |
| ---------- | ------------------- | -------- |
| Infringement remedies | Damages, enhanced damages, injunctions, attorney fees in exceptional cases | 35 U.S.C. §§ 271, 281–285 |
| Portfolio assertion | Multiple patents in series or parallel raise defense cost independent of any one patent's strength | 35 U.S.C. generally |
| Venue and joinder | Historical concentration in plaintiff-friendly districts; post-*TC Heartland* residual venue shopping (EDTX/WDTX NPE activity) | 28 U.S.C. § 1400(b); *TC Heartland LLC v. Kraft Foods*, 581 U.S. 376 (2017) |
| Subject-matter eligibility | § 101 / *Alice* invalidation is a cheaper off-ramp for some defendants and a higher bar for software-heavy small patentees | 35 U.S.C. § 101; *Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank*, 573 U.S. 208 (2014) |
| PTAB review | IPR/PGR as a parallel validity track; cost still substantial; more usable by well-resourced defendants | 35 U.S.C. Chapter 31 |

## Routing

Enabled: civil infringement litigation and demand-letter licensing. There is no general criminal track for garden-variety infringement. The "closed" track here is not criminalization of PAEs; it is practical unavailability of the same extraction model to under-resourced patentees, and practical unavailability of cheap, complete defense to under-resourced operating companies.

Procedural features:

- Defense cost (discovery, experts, claim construction) often exceeds likely settlement.
- A PAE that does not make a product has less exposure to countersuit.
- Fee shifting under § 285 is exceptional, not the default, so weak cases still have negative expected value for defendants.

## Application Evidence

Unified Patents, *Patent Dispute Report: 2023 in Review*: NPEs accounted for **58%** of U.S. patent litigation, similar to the five-year average. NPE (PAE) filings averaged 246 cases per quarter versus 338 for operating companies. NPEs targeted high-tech companies **95%** of the time; non-NPEs targeted high-tech only **14%** of the time. NPEs were responsible for **87.4%** of high-tech litigation. EDTX was the hotspot for NPE activity after Delaware disclosure pressure; WDTX was 54 cases behind.

The FTC's PAE study documents the business of acquiring patents from third parties and generating revenue by asserting them. Large operating companies remain both frequent defendants and frequent plaintiffs; the preferential pattern is resource asymmetry inside a formally symmetric statute, not a one-way street from trolls to industry.

This entry does not claim that every NPE suit is meritless. It claims that the cost structure of 35 U.S.C. litigation, combined with portfolio assertion and venue, enables extraction that small parties cannot symmetrically perform or symmetrically resist.

## Framework notes

Relevant distinction rules from [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md):

- **Rule 4 (Machiavellianism vs Compassion).** Value is extracted because the cost of resistance exceeds the cost of paying, independent of harm to a working product relationship.
- **Rule 15 (Response to loss: retake or expand).** Assertion often claims more than any working invention's lost market; the patent is a lever, not a restoration of a making practice.
- **Rule 22 (Legalism as Idol).** Validity, infringement, and damages are the formal questions. Settlement under cost pressure is the actual disposition.
- **Rule 40 (Safeguards: principled or procedural).** Venue, joinder, IPR, and fee-shifting are procedural. They are usable mainly by parties who can staff them.
- **Rule 42 (Production: distributed or centralized).** Operating production is distributed among firms that ship products. Assertion portfolios concentrate exclusion rights in entities that do not make.

Do not score this entry. The rules name the pattern; they do not replace the instruments or the evidence.
