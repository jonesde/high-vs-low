# Non-competes and trade-secret claims against worker mobility

A worker leaving with general skill is ordinary labor mobility. A large employer can often convert that exit into a TRO, a non-compete suit, an inevitable-disclosure argument, or a Defend Trade Secrets Act claim. Small competitors and individual employees face the same doctrines from the other side: they cannot cheaply buy nationwide injunction practice, and they are the usual defendants.

## Outcome / Goal

The practical result is delayed or blocked job changes, chilled startup formation, and settlements that look like continued control after the employment relationship ends. The parties who obtain it are employers with form agreements, choice-of-law clauses, and the budget for emergency injunctive relief. Workers and small rivals absorb the delay even when they later "win."

## Contrast / Parallel Track

Hiring a competitor's employee, or leaving to do the same kind of work, is competition. Trade-secret law is supposed to police taking of identified secrets, not general skill. Non-competes go further: they restrict work even without a taken secret. The closed analog is a worker or small firm imposing a symmetric restraint on a large employer, or obtaining a fast injunction against a large employer's poaching. Formally possible; practically rare.

## Formal Legal Instruments

| Instrument | Role in the outcome | Citation |
| ---------- | ------------------- | -------- |
| State non-compete statutes / common law | Enforceability tests (reasonableness, legitimate interest, blue pencil); some states ban or narrow | State law; e.g. Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600 |
| Choice-of-law and forum clauses | Pull disputes into employer-friendly states | Contract |
| Defend Trade Secrets Act | Federal civil trade-secret claim; ex parte seizure in extraordinary cases | [18 U.S.C. § 1836](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1836) |
| Inevitable-disclosure doctrine | Injunction on the theory that the worker cannot do the new job without using secrets — not adopted everywhere | State common law |
| NDAs and non-solicits | Functional substitutes when non-competes are weak | Contract |
| FTC Noncompete Rule (2024) | Attempted nationwide ban; **not in effect** | 16 C.F.R. Part 910 (unenforced after *Ryan LLC*) |

**Status of the FTC rule.** The FTC issued a final rule in April 2024 estimating that nearly 30 million U.S. workers (about one in five) were subject to non-competes. On August 20, 2024, the Northern District of Texas in *Ryan LLC v. FTC* barred nationwide enforcement. The Commission later dropped its appeal and shifted to case-by-case enforcement. State statutes remain the live constraint.

## Routing

Enabled: civil injunctions, TROs, and settlement under threat of fees. Closed: a cheap, fast determination that general skill is not a secret, before the worker's new job collapses.

Procedural features:

- Temporary restraining orders issue on paper and speed. The worker's new employer often withdraws rather than fund a fight.
- Choice of law can evade employee-protective states unless a statute blocks it.
- DTSA adds a federal courthouse and, in rare cases, ex parte seizure.
- The employee is usually an individual defendant against a repeat-player plaintiff.

## Application Evidence

FTC 2024: about 30 million workers covered. That is the scale of the contractual overlay, not the number of lawsuits.

Enforcement is uneven by design. California's long-standing public policy against employee non-competes coexists with DTSA and NDA practice and with attempts to use other states' law. Other states enforce reasonableness tests that still allow injunctions against mid-skill workers. Resource asymmetry is the application evidence even where doctrine is facially symmetric: emergency-injunction practice is a product large employers buy.

Recent state and federal reform activity (state bans or wage thresholds; the failed FTC rule; FTC spot enforcement after 2025) is evidence that the default was employer-side enforceability, not a vacuum.

## Framework notes

Relevant distinction rules from [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md):

- **Rule 6 (Individual autonomy and self-determination).** The worker's will to leave and work is subordinated to a prior form contract.
- **Rule 8 (Response to "No").** Quitting, or saying no to a non-compete as a condition of a new job, carries injunctions, fees, and lost offers.
- **Rule 11 (Consensus: voluntary or imposed?).** "Agreement" here is often a condition of getting or keeping work, not a negotiated consensus.
- **Rule 22 (Legalism as Idol).** Reasonableness tests and legitimate-interest language look like limits. The TRO calendar is the operating law.
- **Rule 33 (Dominion: stewardship or ownership).** The employer claims ownership of the worker's future labor, not stewardship of an identified secret.
- **Rule 42 (Production: distributed or centralized).** Independent practice and new firms are distributed production. Non-competes centralize who may produce.

Do not score this entry. The rules name the pattern; they do not replace the instruments or the evidence.
