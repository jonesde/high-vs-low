# Civil asset forfeiture with low contestation

Law enforcement can seize cash, vehicles, and other property through civil and administrative forfeiture without a criminal conviction. Owners with little money, little legal knowledge, or little time rarely recover it. Analogous deprivations of property between private parties require a lawsuit, a higher practical barrier to taking, and no police-kept proceeds.

## Outcome / Goal

The practical result is transfer of property from the person who possessed it to a government forfeiture fund, often shared with the seizing agency. The parties who obtain it are federal, state, and local law enforcement agencies and prosecutors. Owners who are never charged with a related crime, or who are charged and not convicted, still lose the property if they do not or cannot contest.

## Contrast / Parallel Track

Criminal forfeiture follows a conviction and is tied to the defendant's property interest in crime proceeds or instrumentalities. A private party who wants another person's cash or car must sue in conversion, replevin, or contract, prove a claim, and does not keep a "share" as an institutional incentive. Civil forfeiture inverts that: the action is against the property (*in re* the cash or the car), the civil burden is lower than beyond a reasonable doubt, and in many jurisdictions agencies keep a large fraction of what they take.

## Routing

Enabled: civil *in rem* forfeiture and administrative forfeiture. Closed, for most seized property: a criminal conviction as a condition of keeping the property.

Procedural features that produce the split:

- Preponderance (civil), not beyond a reasonable doubt.
- The case is captioned against the property. The owner is a claimant, not a criminal defendant with appointed counsel as of right.
- Administrative forfeiture defaults if no claim is filed. Filing a claim, posting bond where required, hiring counsel, and appearing repeatedly are the real filters.
- Across 24 states in IJ's fourth *Policing for Profit* report, half of currency forfeitures were worth less than $1,678, against an estimated $3,300 cost to hire an attorney. Contesting is often worth less than the property.

## Application Evidence

**Volume.** Institute for Justice, *Policing for Profit 4* (2026): forfeiture has generated at least $82 billion nationwide since 2000. Since 2014 the federal government has forfeited $2 billion to $3 billion per year; 34 states forfeited $300 million to $350 million per year in the periods IJ could measure.

**No hearing.** Among DOJ forfeitures, 71% were administrative. In three states with data, 62% to 76% of seizures were forfeited by default. For owners who do reach a judge, known pre-hearing deadlines in half the states add up to about 188 days.

**Who is seized from, and who contests.** IJ's Philadelphia survey (*Frustrating, Corrupt, Unfair*, 2021): about two-thirds of respondents were Black; 63% earned under $50,000; 18% were unemployed. About 1 in 4 respondents was found or pleaded guilty to related wrongdoing, yet 69% saw the property forfeited. Owners earning under $50,000 were almost 70% less likely even to try to contest. ACLU of Pennsylvania (2015): 63% of Philadelphia cash forfeitures involved money taken from Black residents (43% of city population); 71% of those cash seizures were forfeited without a conviction.

Concentration in lower-income and higher-minority areas is also reported in studies and local data from Chicago, South Carolina, and California. The mechanism is the same: cash-carrying, highly policed neighborhoods, plus a contestation cost that exceeds the median seizure.

## Framework notes

Relevant distinction rules from [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md):

- **Rule 7 (Individual Domain).** The seizure takes the owner's domain. Restoration is conditional on surviving a process designed around default, not around making the owner whole.
- **Rule 9 (Who defines harm).** The seizing authority defines the property as tainted. The owner's account of legitimate cash or an innocent vehicle is an affirmative climb, not the starting point.
- **Rule 13 (Subject or object).** The caption against the property recasts the person as a claimant attached to an object.
- **Rule 15 (Response to loss: retake or expand).** Agencies take more than any demonstrated loss to a victim; proceeds fund the seizing institutions.
- **Rule 22 (Legalism as Idol).** CAFRA's notice and innocent-owner text look like protection. Administrative default and cost barriers are what actually dispose of most property.
- **Rule 36 (Treatment of the Least).** Lower-income and less-educated owners are the least able to contest and the most likely to lose the property.
- **Rule 40 (Safeguards: principled or procedural).** Deadlines, bonds, and hearings are procedural safeguards that function as filters, not as principled limits on taking.

Do not score this entry. The rules name the pattern; they do not replace the instruments or the evidence.
