# Hardrock mining location and patent under the 1872 Act

The General Mining Law of 1872 declared valuable mineral deposits in land belonging to the United States "free and open to exploration and purchase." A locator who discovers a locatable mineral (historically gold, silver, copper, and other hardrock) can hold a claim and, when patenting is available, buy the land and minerals for $2.50 (placer) or $5.00 (lode) an acre. There is no federal royalty on those minerals. The analog is the mineral remaining a public commons, or a lease with a royalty as oil and gas were moved to in 1920.

## Outcome / Goal

The practical result is private title to a slice of the public domain's locatable minerals, at a 19th-century statutory price, without a royalty to the residual public. The parties who obtain it are locators and the firms that buy claims. Later users of the same land (grazing, watershed, unclaimed mineral) take the residual. Patenting has been subject to a long appropriations moratorium; location and unpatented claims still do the work.

## Contrast / Parallel Track

Unclaimed public mineral as a commons anyone may use without exclusive title. Royalty leasing of oil, gas, coal, phosphate, and sodium under the Mineral Leasing Act of 1920 ([pla-033](public-land-mineral-leasing.md)). Homestead-scale surface entry without the mineral estate ([pla-036](land-grants-and-tariff-incidence.md)).

## Formal Legal Instruments

| Instrument | Role in the outcome | Citation |
| ---------- | ------------------- | -------- |
| General Mining Law of 1872 | Location, claim maintenance, patent of locatable minerals on public domain land open to mineral entry | Act of May 10, 1872; 30 U.S.C. ch. 2 |
| Patent prices | $2.50/acre placer; $5.00/acre lode | 1872 Act |
| No federal hardrock royalty | Unlike MLA leasable minerals | Contrast 30 U.S.C. § 181 et seq. |
| Mineral Leasing Act 1920 | Removed oil, gas, coal, oil shale, phosphate, sodium from the claim-patent system | 30 U.S.C. § 181 |

**First receiver:** the locator who perfects the claim (and the patentee, when patents issue). **Delayed end:** the public residual owner who receives no royalty on hardrock; later claimants of the same ground.

## Routing

Enabled: location plus exclusive right to mine; patent when not moratorium-blocked. Closed: a general public royalty or a competitive lease for gold/silver/copper on the public domain.

BLM still administers claims under the 1872 framework. The 1920 Act is the confession that Congress knew how to switch some minerals onto a lease/royalty track and left hardrock on the older, more generous track.

## Application Evidence

BLM: the 1872 law "declared all valuable mineral deposits in land belonging to the United States to be free and open to exploration and purchase" and provides the opportunity to "purchase certain valuable mineral deposits on federal lands ... open to mineral entry." Taxpayer.net / CRS-style fact sheets: patent prices remain $2.50 and $5.00 an acre; patents convey land and minerals; patents are not required to mine. The statutory price, not the market value of the metal, is the legally set transfer.

This is not "mining is expensive so only firms can do it." It is a **title and royalty rule** that stands between the residual public and the mineral even when a small locator could in principle stake a claim. Concentration of claims in well-counseled firms is application evidence of who can staff the paper, not of a missing statute.

## Framework notes

Relevant distinction rules from [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md):

- **Rule 7 (Individual Domain).** The public domain's mineral is moved into a private domain at a statutory price.
- **Rule 15 (Response to loss: retake or expand).** Patent at $5/acre expands the claimant beyond a use right.
- **Rule 33 (Dominion: stewardship or ownership).** Ownership of the mineral, not a royalty stewardship to the residual public.
- **Rule 42 (Production: distributed or centralized).** Location is formally open; paper and capital concentrate who holds claims.
