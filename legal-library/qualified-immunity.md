# Qualified immunity for officials

42 U.S.C. § 1983 lets a person sue a state or local official who, under color of law, deprives them of a federal right. *Harlow v. Fitzgerald* (1982) added a judge-made overlay: officials performing discretionary functions are shielded from civil damages unless they violated "clearly established" statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known. Later cases require a close factual match. The analog is a private person sued for the same blow, the same entry, or the same false statement: no "clearly established" filter, and often no indemnifying city.

This is the liability enabler for the Drug War raid ([pla-040](militarized-local-enforcement.md)), the mandate desk ([pla-043](funding-conditioned-mandates.md)), and other protected-complex officers. *Pearson v. Callahan* (2009), which let courts skip the constitutional-merits step and decide immunity first, was itself a narcotics-task-force search.

## Outcome / Goal

The practical result is that many constitutional claims die at summary judgment even if a jury might find a violation. The parties who obtain it are individual officers, jailers, school officials, and other public employees, plus the municipalities that indemnify them. Injured people keep the statute on paper and lose the damages action in practice. Injunctive claims and *Monell* municipal-policy suits are different tracks; they are harder to plead and do not put the officer's conduct to a damages jury.

## Contrast / Parallel Track

A private security guard or neighbor who uses the same force faces ordinary tort and, if the facts fit, a crime. A municipality under *Monell* has no qualified immunity, but the plaintiff must prove a policy, custom, or failure-to-train — a different and higher structural showing. Prosecutors and judges have **absolute** immunity for the core of their work. The closed analog is a damages trial on whether this raid, this shot, or this detention violated the Constitution, without a prior-case clone.

**Other complex:** the Federal Tort Claims Act discretionary-function exception (28 U.S.C. § 2680(a)) and the Westfall Act substitution of the United States for federal employees (28 U.S.C. § 2679) are the federal-employee cousins. *Boyle v. United Technologies* government-contractor defense is the MIC cousin: the designated maker of a military product shares a slice of the government's immunity. PREP ([pla-042](covered-person-liability-shields.md)) is the health-complex cousin for countermeasure makers.

§ 1983's text has no immunity clause. *Harlow* is judicial gloss. That is the preferential design: a Reconstruction civil-rights statute plus a 1982 overlay that filters most damages claims.

## Routing

Enabled: official's motion to dismiss or for summary judgment on qualified immunity; interlocutory appeal of a denial. Closed: ordinary damages trial on the constitutional facts unless a prior case put a reasonable official on notice with similar facts.

Criminal prosecution of an officer remains theoretically open. It is a different decision-maker (a prosecutor who works with the same agencies) and a higher proof burden. The civil overlay is what this page names.

## Application Evidence

*Harlow*'s holding is the mechanism. *Pearson* is Drug War application: Central Utah Narcotics Task Force entry, immunity first.

Joanna Schwartz, *Police Indemnification*, 89 N.Y.U. L. Rev. 885 (2014): in 2006–2011, officers in 44 of the 70 largest agencies paid **0.02%** of dollars awarded in police-misconduct suits; governments paid the rest. Scope: those agencies, those years, indemnification — not a national grant-rate. Reuters (2014–2018 excessive-force QI rulings in California and Texas district courts): Texas granted immunity in 59% of the sampled cases, California in 34%. Scope: two states, excessive force, those years.

The Institute for Justice *Unaccountable* appellate dataset is the large circuit-court collection. Cite it for **who is a defendant** (not only police) rather than a single invented national win-rate.
