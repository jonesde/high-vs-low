# Legal tender and forced acceptance of government and Fed notes

United States coins and currency, "including Federal reserve notes and circulating notes of Federal reserve banks and national banks," are legal tender for all debts, public charges, taxes, and dues. Foreign gold or silver coins are not. A creditor or tax office must treat the privileged ticket as discharging the obligation. The analog is a contract that names a specific commodity, or a foreign coin, which the statute strips of tender status.

## Outcome / Goal

The practical result is a legally forced market for whatever the United States and the Reserve Banks have issued. First receivers of new notes can retire debts and taxes at face value immediately. Parties who contracted in gold, silver, or some other unit, or who refuse the notes, lose the enforcement of that unit. The issuer and its first counterparties do not have to bid the notes into acceptance; the code bids for them.

## Contrast / Parallel Track

A private contract payable in a named weight of metal; payment in foreign coin; a tax receivable only in specie. *Knox v. Lee* (Legal Tender Cases, 79 U.S. 457 (1871)) upheld Congress's power to make paper a tender for private debts. 31 U.S.C. § 5103 is the current sentence. FRA § 16 already made Fed notes receivable for taxes. Together they close the analog of walking away from the privileged ticket.

## Formal Legal Instruments

| Instrument | Role in the outcome | Citation |
| ---------- | ------------------- | -------- |
| Current legal-tender statute | US coins and currency, including Fed notes and national-bank notes, are tender for debts, taxes, and dues; foreign gold or silver coins are not | [31 U.S.C. § 5103](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5103) |
| Fed notes as tax receivable | Notes "receivable ... for all taxes, customs, and other public dues" | FRA § 16 |
| Legal Tender Cases | Congress may make paper a tender for private debts | *Knox v. Lee*, 79 U.S. 457 (1871); *Juilliard v. Greenman*, 110 U.S. 421 (1884) |
| Gold-clause resolution | 1933 joint resolution voided gold clauses in public and private contracts | 48 Stat. 112 (June 5, 1933) |

**First receiver:** whoever already holds the tender (Treasury, member banks, later dealers) can extinguish obligations at par before street prices have fully moved. **Delayed end:** a wage or price that has not yet been rewritten in the new unit; a gold-clause creditor after 1933.

## Routing

Enabled: discharge of debt and tax in the privileged unit whether or not the creditor wanted that unit. Closed: judicial enforcement of a competing unit for "all debts, public charges, taxes, and dues."

Tender is not a requirement that a shop post a price in dollars, but it is a requirement that a dollar debt be extinguishable in the issued notes. Combined with tax receivable status, it is the demand that makes issue valuable on day one.

## Application Evidence

The statute is short and structural. § 5103 names Fed notes expressly. Foreign metallic coin is named as *not* tender. The 1933 gold-clause resolution is the same logic applied to existing contracts: the privileged unit overrides the contracted unit.

This page does not claim that every seller must accept cash for a new sale. It claims that the **stock of debts and taxes** — the largest forced market — is legally reserved to the issued ticket. That is what lets a first receiver of new notes retire real claims before dilution shows up in wages.
