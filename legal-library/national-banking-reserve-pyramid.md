# National banking, reserve pyramids, and inelastic interior currency

From 1863 until the Federal Reserve Act, the United States legally assigned note issue to banks that bought federal bonds and parked reserves up a pyramid that ended in New York. Interior banks and ordinary holders of state-bank notes sat at the delayed end. Harvest demand and panics (1893, 1907) showed the inelasticity. The 1908 emergency-currency act and the Pujo "money trust" record are the confession that the privilege was already concentrated before 1913.

## Outcome / Goal

The practical result is a legally designated first receiver of the circulating medium: a national bank that holds Treasury bonds as the backing for notes, and a central-reserve-city bank that holds other banks' reserves. New York correspondents get the interior's cash in ordinary times and ration it in panics. Farmers, country banks, and holders of taxed-out state notes get the residual.

## Contrast / Parallel Track

A state-chartered bank issuing its own notes against local assets; an interior community holding specie without sending it to a correspondent; a silver or gold coin whose statutory definition is not rewritten to drop one metal. The National Bank Acts and the 1873 coinage statute close those analogs.

## Formal Legal Instruments

| Instrument | Role in the outcome | Citation |
| ---------- | ------------------- | -------- |
| National Currency Act / National Bank Act | Federal charter; notes issued against US bonds (up to 90% of holdings); OCC | Acts of Feb. 25, 1863 and June 3, 1864 |
| Tax on state-bank notes | 2% then 10% tax that drove state notes out of circulation | 1865–66 amendments |
| Three-tier reserve pyramid | Country banks, reserve cities, central reserve cities (NY, later Chicago and St. Louis); vault gold 25% in the center | 1864 Act reserve schedule |
| Coinage Act of 1873 | Dropped the standard silver dollar from free coinage; gold as the remaining full-bodied unit | Act of Feb. 12, 1873 |
| Aldrich-Vreeland Act | Emergency national-bank currency in a crisis; National Monetary Commission | Act of May 30, 1908 |
| Clearinghouse loan certificates | Private New York/Chicago certificates used as reserve substitutes in panics — the extra-legal first-receiver tool the 1908 Act partly legalized | Clearinghouse practice, 1873–1907 |

**First receiver:** the national bank that buys bonds and issues notes, and the central-reserve correspondent that holds the pyramid's cash. **Delayed end:** country banks calling reserves in harvest season; note holders in a run.

## Routing

Enabled: federal charter plus bond-backed note issue plus legally required reserve deposits in designated cities. Closed: competing state-note issue (taxed away); elastic interior currency without going through New York.

The National Monetary Commission and the Aldrich Plan (Jekyll Island 1910, reported 1911–12) and the Pujo Committee (1912–13) are the official record that the 1907 rationing was treated as a structural defect to be solved by a new central issue privilege, not by dismantling the pyramid.

## Application Evidence

Federal Reserve History and OCC accounts: national banks had to hold reserves against notes and deposits; country banks could count balances at reserve-city banks; reserve-city banks could count balances in New York. Sprague (1910) and later Fed histories name "pyramiding" as the defect that pulled interior cash to Wall Street and froze it there in panics.

The 1873 Act is a legally chosen unit, not a market accident: Congress stopped free coinage of the standard silver dollar. Holders of silver-producing claims and bimetallic contracts took the delayed, redefined end. Gold-clause and gold-holding parties sat nearer the remaining unit.

Aldrich-Vreeland emergency currency (used in 1914) and clearinghouse certificates are evidence that extra issue was already a **privilege of the existing note-issuing and clearing core**, not of the street economy.
