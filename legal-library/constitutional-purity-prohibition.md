# Constitutional purity prohibition

The Eighteenth Amendment (ratified 1919, effective 1920) forbade manufacture, sale, and transportation of intoxicating liquors for beverage purposes. The Volstead Act defined "intoxicating" at 0.5% alcohol and built the federal enforcement machine. The Twenty-First Amendment (1933) repealed the ban and left the field to the states. The analog is ordinary adult consumption of a fermented or distilled product. The preferential fact is not that alcohol is harmless. It is that **purity/abstinence was written into the Constitution** as a crime for the street, while medicinal, sacramental, and industrial exceptions remained for designated holders.

This page is the template for later purity tools: Harrison through the CSA, then health-emergency compulsion. Other complexes use the same shape when they criminalize a common practice and exempt the franchisee.

## Outcome / Goal

The practical result is federal criminalization of a mass market. The parties who obtain it are the temperance organizations that captured the amendment process, the Prohibition Bureau (Treasury), and the exception-holders (physicians writing whiskey, churches, industry). Ordinary sellers and drinkers take the criminal track. Repeal moved the franchise back to state liquor authorities, not to an unregulated commons.

## Contrast / Parallel Track

Pre-Volstead licensed saloons and home production. Medicinal whiskey under permit. Sacramental wine. Industrial alcohol (denatured). Those tracks stayed open. The closed analog is the unlicensed beverage trade, enforced hardest in cities and against poorer and immigrant neighborhoods — the same class gradient later seen in the Drug War ([pla-039](mandatory-minimum-quantity-disparity.md)).

**Other complex:** wartime and sedition acts that criminalize ordinary speech for the public while official propaganda remains privileged. The purity tool is legalistic control of a common behavior, not a harm-specific tort.

## Formal Legal Instruments

| Instrument | Role in the outcome | Citation |
| ---------- | ------------------- | -------- |
| Eighteenth Amendment | National ban on beverage liquor manufacture, sale, transport | U.S. Const. amend. XVIII (1920–1933) |
| Volstead Act | 0.5% definition; enforcement; exceptions | National Prohibition Act, 41 Stat. 305 (1919) |
| Twenty-First Amendment | Repeal; state control of liquor | U.S. Const. amend. XXI (1933) |
| Treasury / Bureau of Prohibition | Federal police for a consumption crime | Organic enforcement under Volstead |

## Routing

Enabled: federal crime for the unlicensed beverage trade; permit tracks for designated uses. Closed: treating adult alcohol commerce as a state licensing or tort problem only.

The amendment is the confession: ordinary majoritarian criminal law was not enough; purity required a constitutional lock so a later Congress could not easily undo it. Repeal is evidence the lock could still be broken — and that the Drug War chose statutes and schedules instead of a new amendment ([pla-038](tax-schedule-as-crime.md)).

## Application Evidence

Volstead's 0.5% line is arbitrary relative to intoxication. Exception permits are the preferential split. Enforcement capacity never matched the market; selective raids and corruption are the historical application. Repeal did not restore a free market: the Twenty-First Amendment became a new state-franchise machine for liquor control.

Do not treat "Prohibition failed" as the library's holding. Treat **who was exempt** and **who was raided** as the preferential pattern that the Drug War kept.
