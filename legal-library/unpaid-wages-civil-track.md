# Employer retention of unpaid wages on the civil track

Employers who fail to pay wages owed are steered into civil and administrative recovery, where the typical result is (at most) back pay plus limited add-ons. Analogous taking of property by a customer or employee above a low dollar threshold is routinely charged as theft.

## Outcome / Goal

The practical result is retention, delay, or underpayment of wages with little criminal exposure. The parties who obtain it are employers, including firms that underpay as a recurring cost of doing business. Workers may later recover some unpaid amounts through the Department of Labor, a state labor agency, or a private suit, but recovery is a small share of estimated losses and does not impose the felony record, incarceration risk, or restitution-plus-punishment package that ordinary theft carries.

## Contrast / Parallel Track

Taking goods or cash of comparable value from a store or employer is charged under state larceny, shoplifting, and embezzlement statutes. Pew's review of felony theft thresholds found that since 2000 at least 37 states raised those thresholds, with common cutoffs of $500, $1,000, or $2,000; some states remain lower. A first-time shoplifting case above the local felony line is a criminal case. A first-time FLSA minimum-wage or overtime violation of much larger aggregate value is a civil case. See [employee-theft-criminal-track.md](employee-theft-criminal-track.md) for the inverted pattern.

| State payday and minimum-wage statutes | Parallel civil/administrative tracks; some states add criminal wage-theft provisions that remain rarely used relative to ordinary theft | State labor codes; see EPI prosecutor survey |

The criminal hook exists on paper. The civil track is the one that runs.

## Routing

Enabled: civil private actions, DOL and state administrative recovery, occasional civil money penalties, and non-action (most underpayment is never detected). Closed, in practice: federal criminal prosecution under § 216(a).

Procedural features that produce the split:

- Detection is complaint-driven or inspection-driven, with a low chance of random inspection.
- First-time nonwillful violators typically owe back wages; liquidated damages and CMPs are extra and historically uncommon.
- Criminal prosecution requires willfulness, a referral, and a prosecutor. Imprisonment under the FLSA requires a prior conviction under § 216(a).
- Ordinary theft does not require an agency referral. Police and prosecutors charge it directly against the person who took the property.

State experiments that recast wage theft as larceny (California AB 1003, New York, Colorado, Minnesota, and local DA initiatives) are exceptions. They confirm the default federal and historical state routing rather than replacing it.

## Application Evidence

**Loss estimates (scope labeled).** Cooper and Kroeger (EPI, 2017), using CPS data for the ten most populous states, estimated that 2.4 million workers in those states lost about $8 billion annually to *minimum-wage violations alone* (about $3,300 per year-round affected worker). If those states are representative, the national figure for that one form of wage theft exceeds $15 billion per year. Broader "all forms of wage theft" estimates (overtime, off-the-clock, tip theft, illegal deductions) are higher; figures around $50 billion per year refer to that wider bundle, not to the same $15 billion statistic.

**Recovery vs. loss.** WHD reported recovering more than $259 million in back wages for 176,957 employees in fiscal year 2025. That recovery is two orders of magnitude below even the narrower $15 billion minimum-wage-violation estimate. NIWR notes that less than 3% of estimated stolen wages is recovered in a typical year.

**Criminal track.** Stansbury (IZA DP 16882, summarized by MIT Sloan and CEPR): 38 criminal convictions for FLSA minimum-wage or overtime violations from 1994 to 2020; fines in four of those cases (mean about $3,063); no prison sentences in that series. Among willful violations DOL detected, the chance of a criminal conviction was under 0.7%. For 2005–2016, DOL found thousands of willful FLSA violations against a handful of criminal convictions.

**Incentive structure.** Stansbury estimates that a typical U.S. firm would need a 48–83% chance of DOL detection (or about a 25% chance of a successful private FLSA suit) to make compliance the rational choice under existing penalties. Observed detection for high-violation sectors is far lower (on the order of 1% for targeted investigations of large fast-food chains in her example).

## Framework notes

Relevant distinction rules from [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md):

- **Rule 1 (Pride vs Humility and Equality).** The same taking of value is a crime when the taker is a customer or employee and a recoverable debt when the taker is an employer. Status, not the act, selects the track.
- **Rule 7 (Individual Domain).** Unpaid wages are a taking from the worker's domain. The civil track treats that taking as an account to be settled if someone sues, not as a violation of the person.
- **Rule 22 (Legalism as Idol).** FLSA § 216(a) exists; the hedge of civil procedure, agency discretion, and willfulness-plus-prior-conviction for imprisonment is what actually governs. People serve the procedure.
- **Rule 25 (Justice: restorative or punitive).** Ordinary theft is punitive. Wage underpayment, when addressed at all, is billed as restorative (back wages) without the punitive overlay applied to the lower-status taker — and usually without even full restoration.
- **Rule 36 (Treatment of the Least).** Low-wage workers, the parties with the least capacity to sue or wait, absorb the residual loss.
- **Rule 38 (Consequences: natural or artificial).** The felony consequence for shoplifting is an artificial, authority-imposed penalty. The consequence for wage underpayment is often no imposed penalty at all, which is itself an artificial exemption from the theft frame.

Do not score this entry. The rules name the pattern; they do not replace the instruments or the evidence.
