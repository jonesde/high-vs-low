# Purchased germplasm reuse restricted by patents and bag tags

A farmer has land, equipment, and a paid bag of seed. Saving a portion of the harvest to plant next season is the old technique. Utility patents, Plant Variety Protection, and bag-tag licenses close it. This is not farm credit and not the price of seed. It is a use ban on a technique the buyer is fully equipped to perform.

## Outcome / Goal

The practical result is single-season use of patented germplasm and a ban on independent replanting and breeding from the purchased seed. The parties who obtain it are seed companies that sell under limited licenses. Farmers who try to replant — including from a grain elevator, as in *Bowman* — are infringement defendants.

## Contrast / Parallel Track

For most of agricultural history, saving seed was ordinary use of the harvested crop. Plant Variety Protection (1970) already limited saving and sales of protected varieties, with a farmer's-exemption that utility patents later overrode in practice. A public-domain or unpatented variety can still be saved. The closed analog is using the natural reproductive capacity of a plant the farmer grew on the farmer's land.

## Formal Legal Instruments

| Instrument | Role in the outcome | Citation |
| ---------- | ------------------- | -------- |
| Utility patents on seed, plants, and traits | Exclusive right to make, use, sell; replanting treated as "making" a new copy | 35 U.S.C. § 101, § 271; *Diamond v. Chakrabarty*, 447 U.S. 303 (1980); *J.E.M. Ag Supply v. Pioneer Hi-Bred*, 534 U.S. 124 (2001) |
| Patent exhaustion does not cover replanting | Exhaustion lets the buyer use or resell **that** seed, not reproduce it | *Bowman v. Monsanto Co.*, 569 U.S. 278 (2013) |
| Plant Variety Protection Act | Variety certificates; limited saved-seed privilege that utility patents do not copy | 7 U.S.C. §§ 2321 et seq. |
| Bag-tag / technology agreements | Contract: one season, no saving, no supply to others for planting | Point-of-sale licenses |

## Routing

Enabled: civil patent infringement plus contract. Closed: first-sale / exhaustion as a complete defense to replanting.

*Bowman* is explicit. Vernon Bowman bought commodity soybeans from a grain elevator (second-generation grain, mixed lots that included Roundup Ready progeny), planted them, sprayed glyphosate, saved, and replanted. He had paid. He had land and equipment. The Court unanimously held that planting is **making** a new patented article. Exhaustion "leaves untouched the patentee's ability to prevent a buyer from making new copies."

## Application Evidence

The holding is the evidence. After *Bowman*, a farmer who is fully resourced to save seed still infringes by doing so when the trait is patented.

PVPA's saved-seed provision shows Congress knew the analog and carved a limited privilege for *certificate* varieties. *J.E.M.* allowed utility patents to sit on top of PVPA, so the narrower PVPA privilege does not rescue a utility-patented trait. Bag-tag contracts add a license theory even where exhaustion arguments are tried.

Do not cite seed *price* as the restriction. Bowman could buy commodity grain. He could not use it as seed.
