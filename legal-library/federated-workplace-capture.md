# Federated workplace capture

Cleveland's Federation for Charity and Philanthropy (1913) ran one campaign for member agencies chosen with the Chamber of Commerce. That **federated, united ask** spread as Community Chests, War Chests, and United Funds: one drive, pooled gifts, a committee that allocates. **United Way of America** is the later named franchise of the same method, not the origin. Employers then offered a **payroll-deduction default** to the federation. Combined Federal Campaign (5 C.F.R. Part 950) is the federal workplace analog. The analog is cash to a named local shop or a person. The worker's gift is allocated by a committee neither the giver nor the recipient sits on.

Controllers: employers who sign exclusive or default deduction agreements; allocation committees; the national office once the method is branded. The local agency is the face. State charitable-solicitation registration adds a barrier to competing small asks.

Before the workplace card, the method still ran on **performance and exposure as the fundraising technology**, not as color commentary: fame and status were how the federated ask reached people who would later be reached by payroll default. Cleveland's 1914 campaign used a talking motion picture of Andrew Carnegie on "the duties of a man of wealth." Mid-century radio and film stars lent the same federated ask a face: Community Chest Red Feather kickoffs on the networks (Truman opened the national Chest campaigns from the White House, 1949–51); the Hollywood Victory Committee's war-relief and charity broadcasts (thousands of appearances); Eddie Cantor's radio March of Dimes (1938) and a documented 1951 Community Chest Red Feather appearance. Nat King Cole sat in that movement as a performer who gave exposure — NAACP membership and benefits, Montgomery aid, the 1963 March — not as a principal of the payroll franchise. UNCF (founded 1944) is a parallel federated college chest of the same years. William Aramony (United Way of America president 1970–92; convicted 1995 on fraud and tax counts) is the later **centralized** application: a national CEO, spin-off companies, payroll millions. The receipts that carry the page remain Cleveland 1913, CFC, Aramony, and payroll share.

This page does not decide whether federated fundraising is efficient. It names who captures the workplace gift.

## Outcome / Goal

The practical result is a steered stream. The parties who obtain it are federations with payroll access and the employers who want one campaign. Independent local charities and analog persons take the residual: they are not on the deduction card.

## Contrast / Parallel Track

A worker handing cash to a food pantry. A designated gift that the federation actually honors (some campaigns allow designations — a crack, not the default). The closed analog is payroll access for any 501(c)(3) the worker names, or no workplace intermediary at all.

Cleveland 1913 is organizational, not a federal statute. The preferential design is **contract plus default**: one card, one campaign.

## Routing

Enabled: exclusive/default deduction; CFC listing; federation allocation. Closed: analog local shop on the same card without federation membership.

## Application Evidence

United Way-associated figures that **57%** of UW donations come through payroll deductions and about 20% from corporate gifts: scope-label as UW/encyclopedia reporting, not an IRS census. Aramony: Washington Post 1992 salary reporting (~$463,000 pay and benefits); 1995 conviction on multiple felony counts including fraud and tax. That is national-office centralization of the method, not the 1913 chest.

UNCF 1944 first campaign (~$760,000 for member colleges, per UNCF history) is contemporaneous federated form. Carnegie 1914 talking picture: United Way Cleveland / VCU Social Welfare History Project. Truman Community Chest radio: Truman Library public papers (e.g. 30 Sept. 1949; 29 Sept. 1950; 30 Sept. 1951). Hollywood Victory Committee: National WWII Museum summary of 7,700 events, 4,147 stars, 390 broadcasts for war relief and charity. Cantor: March of Dimes origin (National Foundation, 1938 radio appeal); Red Feather presentation 11 Sept. 1951 (contemporaneous press). Cole: NAACP/March facts as exposure, not as a United Way officer.
