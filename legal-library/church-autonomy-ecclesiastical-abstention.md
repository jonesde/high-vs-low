# Church autonomy and ecclesiastical abstention

*Watson v. Jones* (1871) arose from a Presbyterian split after the Civil War. The Supreme Court held that civil courts have no ecclesiastical jurisdiction over questions of faith, discipline, and church government, and that they must accept the decision of the highest tribunal in a hierarchical church. Later cases (*Kedroff*, *Serbian Eastern Orthodox*) constitutionalized that abstention. The analog is a secular club, school, or employer sued for the same battery, fraud, or negligent retention: the court hears the claim. Framed as "internal church business," the same facts are often dismissed.

This page is the post-bellum start of the franchise. Belief, expression, and receipt were the original First Amendment objects. Autonomy doctrine protects the **institution's** power to define and dispose of members.

This page does not decide which side of a schism was theologically correct. It names who gets a courthouse and who does not.

## Outcome / Goal

The practical result is a justiciability wall. The parties who obtain it are hierarchical churches and other religious organizations that can caption a dispute as doctrine or discipline. Members, employees, and third parties who were harmed take the residual: no merits hearing, or a hearing stripped of the personnel file ([pla-055](clergy-privilege-discovery-walls.md)).

## Contrast / Parallel Track

A union, fraternal lodge, or corporation sued for assault, defamation, or covering up a known abuser. *Jones v. Wolf* (1979) allows "neutral principles" of property law in some church-property fights — a crack, not a general tort docket. The closed analog is treating a beating, a fraud, or a child's injury as a civil wrong regardless of the caption on the building.

**Other complex:** qualified immunity ([pla-045](qualified-immunity.md)) — another inside track that is not a jury. Health-complex PREP ([pla-042](covered-person-liability-shields.md)) is statutory product immunity; this page is judge-made institutional immunity.

## Formal Legal Instruments

| Instrument | Role in the outcome | Citation |
| ---------- | ------------------- | -------- |
| *Watson v. Jones* | Civil courts accept hierarchical church tribunals on faith, discipline, and government | *Watson v. Jones*, 80 U.S. 679 (1871) |
| *Kedroff* | Autonomy as Free Exercise / Fourteenth Amendment, not only federal common law | *Kedroff v. St. Nicholas Cathedral*, 344 U.S. 94 (1952) |
| *Serbian Eastern Orthodox* | Courts may not second-guess ecclesiastical removal of a bishop | *Serbian Eastern Orthodox Diocese v. Milivojevich*, 426 U.S. 696 (1976) |
| *Jones v. Wolf* | Neutral-principles option for **property** deeds and trusts, not a general tort path | *Jones v. Wolf*, 443 U.S. 595 (1979) |

*Watson* is Reconstruction-era: the Court steps out of a church split that tracked the war. The preferential design is older than RFRA. RFRA ([pla-058](rfra-commercial-overlay.md)) later adds a statutory overlay on top of this wall.

## Routing

Enabled: motion to dismiss for ecclesiastical abstention / church autonomy; deference to the church's own tribunal. Closed: ordinary civil merits on battery, fraud, or negligent supervision when the defendant can recast the facts as discipline or doctrine.

## Application Evidence

*Watson*'s holding is the mechanism. Circuit splits on whether autonomy bars **negligent-supervision** claims for clergy abuse (some dismiss; some allow secular-duty claims) are application evidence that the wall is the fight. Scope-label by circuit and year; do not invent a national dismissal rate.

Shunning and disfellowship cases that die as "internal discipline" are the membership analog. Label alleged facts. The preferential enabler is **who decides whether the court may speak**.
