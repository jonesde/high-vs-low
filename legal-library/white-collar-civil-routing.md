# Large-scale financial wrongdoing on the civil and DPA track

Securities and corporate financial misconduct can be charged as a federal crime. It is often resolved as an SEC settlement, a civil money penalty, disgorgement, a monitorship, or a deferred or non-prosecution agreement. Lower-dollar fraud — benefits, small wire schemes, identity theft of modest sums — is charged criminally as a matter of course. The split is not that executives are never prosecuted. It is that the civil/DPA off-ramp is built for large institutional matters and is not how street-level financial cases are closed.

## Outcome / Goal

The practical result is resolution without a corporate conviction, often without individual convictions, and with penalties that can be large in dollars and small relative to market capitalization. The parties who obtain it are corporations, and frequently their executives, in matters that also involve the SEC, CFTC, or similar regulators. DOJ retains criminal authority and uses it selectively.

## Contrast / Parallel Track

A person who submits false statements to obtain public benefits, or who runs a small investment scam, is typically a criminal defendant. Those cases do not end in an SEC-style neither-admit-nor-deny order or a corporate DPA. The analog conduct is deceit for money. The closed track is the DPA/civil-settlement package for defendants who lack an institution, a compliance department, and parallel regulatory jurisdiction.

This page does not claim that no executive is ever indicted.

## Formal Legal Instruments

| Instrument | Role in the outcome | Citation |
| ---------- | ------------------- | -------- |
| Securities Act / Exchange Act civil enforcement | Injunctions, disgorgement, civil penalties; neither-admit-nor-deny settlements | 15 U.S.C. §§ 77a et seq., 78a et seq. |
| Parallel criminal statutes | Securities fraud, mail/wire fraud, conspiracy — available, used selectively | 15 U.S.C. § 78j(b); 18 U.S.C. §§ 1341, 1343, 1348, 371 |
| Deferred prosecution agreements | Charges filed, held in abeyance on conditions; Speedy Trial Act exclusion | Justice Manual 9-28.000; [18 U.S.C. § 3161(h)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/3161) |
| Non-prosecution agreements | No filed charge; contract with DOJ | Justice Manual 9-28.000 |
| Civil money penalties and monitorships | Regulatory close without a criminal judgment | Agency statutes and settlement terms |

DOJ policy on business organizations explicitly treats NPAs and DPAs as a middle ground between declination and conviction, including to avoid collateral consequences to a firm.

## Routing

Enabled: SEC/CFTC civil process, disgorgement, monitors, DPAs/NPAs. Closed as the default: indictment of the entity and, in many DPA matters, of responsible individuals.

Procedural features:

- Parallel civil and criminal jurisdiction lets prosecutors and regulators divide the case.
- "Collateral consequences" (charter loss, debarment, innocent-employee harm) are arguments available mainly to large firms.
- Cooperation, voluntary disclosure, and ability to pay a headline penalty substitute for a plea.
- Individual street-level defendants have pretrial diversion in some districts; they do not have the corporate DPA market.

## Application Evidence

Brandon Garrett's work on corporate prosecutions: only about one-third of cases in which public companies received DPAs led to criminal prosecutions of individuals; of 21 DPAs in 2018, five involved individual prosecutions; individual prosecutions accompany on the order of 27% of leniency agreements in later tallies. Penalties that look large can be a small fraction of market cap; event studies have found that stock prices often do not fall (and sometimes rise) on announcement of these deals.

SEC enforcement remains a civil-penalty and injunction practice. DOJ corporate criminal resolutions continue, but the existence of the DPA/NPA channel — and its absence from ordinary benefits-fraud dockets — is the routing fact.

USSC and DOJ charging data show that fraud offenses with modest loss amounts occupy a large share of federal criminal fraud caseloads, while the highest-loss corporate matters are the ones most likely to appear in the Gibson Dunn / Stanford / Garrett DPA lists. This page does not merge those datasets into a fake "X times more likely" ratio; the institutional split is the claim.

## Framework notes

Relevant distinction rules from [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md):

- **Rule 1 (Pride vs Humility and Equality).** The same deceit is a case about "markets" and "compliance" when the defendant is a firm, and a case about "fraud" when the defendant is a person without one.
- **Rule 22 (Legalism as Idol).** Collateral-consequence doctrine and settlement procedure become the law that decides whether a crime is named.
- **Rule 25 (Justice: restorative or punitive).** Street-level financial crime is punitive. Institutional financial crime is framed as remedial (disgorgement, monitors) even when victims are many.
- **Rule 28 (Fairness: repair or equal suffering).** Headline penalties are presented as fairness. They do not equalize suffering with ordinary fraud defendants and often do not restore victims first.
- **Rule 36 (Treatment of the Least).** Lower-income benefits and small-fraud defendants are the least, and they receive the criminal track.
- **Rule 40 (Safeguards: principled or procedural).** DPAs, NPAs, and neither-admit-nor-deny orders are procedural off-ramps, not a principled line about culpability.

Do not score this entry. The rules name the pattern; they do not replace the instruments or the evidence.
