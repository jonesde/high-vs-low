# Employee theft of modest value on the criminal track

An employee who takes a few hundred or a few thousand dollars in cash or goods is a theft or embezzlement defendant, often a felon. An employer who underpays wages of larger aggregate value is a civil respondent. This entry is the mirror of [pla-001](unpaid-wages-civil-track.md).

## Outcome / Goal

The practical result is criminal exposure, a record, and often incarceration risk for workers who take modest value from an employer, plus a parallel civil recovery action for the employer. The parties who obtain it are employers (as complaining witnesses and civil plaintiffs) and prosecutors. The employee is on both tracks at once.

## Contrast / Parallel Track

The analog is employer wage theft, tip theft, off-the-clock work, and illegal deductions. Those acts take value from the worker's domain. They run through FLSA and state labor agencies. See [unpaid-wages-civil-track.md](unpaid-wages-civil-track.md). EPI's prosecutor survey states the historical pattern directly: a worker stealing from an employer would likely face charges; an employer committing wage theft likely would not.

Recent state laws that define intentional wage nonpayment as theft (California AB 1003, New York larceny amendments, Colorado, Minnesota felony wage theft) are real. They remain the exception relative to the volume of wage underpayment and relative to the routine charging of employee larceny.

## Formal Legal Instruments

| Instrument | Role in the outcome | Citation |
| ---------- | ------------------- | -------- |
| State larceny / theft | Value-based misdemeanor/felony split; low dollar thresholds | State penal codes |
| Embezzlement / larceny by employee / larceny by bailee | Status as employee is an element or an enhancement | State penal codes |
| Employer civil recovery | Conversion, unjust enrichment, sometimes treble or statutory damages | State civil statutes |
| Restitution as a criminal condition | Makes the worker pay twice: criminal process plus repayment | Sentencing / probation statutes |

Pew (2017): since 2000 at least 37 states raised felony theft thresholds. Common lines are $500, $1,000, or $2,000; some states remain lower (Florida was $300 in that survey; California grand theft is $950). Employee-theft and embezzlement provisions sometimes use different (lower) thresholds or skip the petty-theft option.

## Routing

Enabled: criminal charging as the default police response to an employer's report, with civil recovery in parallel. Closed: non-action, which is the employer's counterpart in pla-001. Also closed, as a practical matter: treating systematic wage underpayment as the same theft.

Procedural features:

- A named complaining business, inventory records, and a defendant who is already identified.
- No requirement of a labor-agency referral.
- Felony thresholds that a single till-skimming episode can clear.
- Prosecutorial familiarity with employee-theft charging packages vs. unfamiliarity with wage-theft as theft.

## Application Evidence

**Thresholds.** Pew documents felony theft cutoffs commonly in the $500–$2,000 range and notes that the dollar line is not correlated with property-crime rates. California: grand theft over $950 (Penal Code § 487); AB 1003 later treated intentional wage theft over $950 from one employee (or $2,350 from multiple employees in a year) as grand theft — an attempt to put employer taking on the same ladder, which is evidence that it was not already there.

**Asymmetry of practice.** Stansbury: 38 FLSA criminal convictions over 1994–2020, no prison in that series. Employee theft of similar or smaller amounts is charged as felony in ordinary state practice. There is no comparable national "38 convictions in 26 years" statistic for employee larceny; the point is that the criminal track is open and used.

**Retail internal theft.** Industry loss-prevention figures treat employee theft as a large share of shrink (one 2026 industry compilation using NRF/Appriss-type sources puts internal theft on the order of tens of billions annually). Those figures measure retailer loss, not charging rates, but they show that employers treat worker taking as a security-and-prosecution problem while wage underpayment is a labor-compliance problem.

## Framework notes

Relevant distinction rules from [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md):

- **Rule 1 (Pride vs Humility and Equality).** The employee's taking is crime. The employer's taking of wages is a dispute. Equality of persons would charge the act, not the roster status.
- **Rule 25 (Justice: restorative or punitive).** Punitive justice is applied downward. Restorative-sounding civil process is applied upward, usually without even restoration.
- **Rule 28 (Fairness: repair or equal suffering).** Fairness here is not repair of harm. It is suffering imposed on the lower-status taker and not on the higher-status taker of larger sums.
- **Rule 36 (Treatment of the Least).** The worker is the least in the employment pair and the one who receives the criminal overlay.
- **Rule 38 (Consequences: natural or artificial).** Felony classification at $500 or $950 is an artificial line. It is enforced against employees and, until recent state experiments, not against employers.

Do not score this entry. The rules name the pattern; they do not replace the instruments or the evidence.
